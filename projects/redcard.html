A man down or a goal down? Pricing a red card in expected points

Question

A red card and a conceded goal are both bad. Which is worse, and when? The answer has to depend on the minute and the score: a man down with 80 minutes left is a long time to suffer; a goal conceded while level in the 85th minute is close to the whole result. The paper prices both in the same unit, expected league points, so they can be compared directly.

Setup

Two public sources: the Kaggle European Soccer Database (2008/09–2015/16) and the Wyscout 2017/18 event data, matched to bookmaker odds from football-data.co.uk as a strength measure. Goals were checked against final scores (1,826 of 1,826 Wyscout matches), red cards against an independent record. Each team-interval of five minutes is one row of a Poisson regression of goals on time, home advantage, strength, score difference and the two red-card indicators; richer models (interactions with score state and strength) were kept only where held-out log-likelihood improved.

Results

  • A team a man down scores at 0.63 of its normal rate; its opponent scores at 1.56. Both are stable to within a few hundredths across nine seasons, league-season fixed effects, dropping the first ten minutes after the card, and dropping matches with two cards.
  • Early in a match a red card costs more than a goal. The sign flips at roughly the 15th minute when the score is level, and later when leading; when leading by one in the 85th minute a conceded goal costs 1.6 points more than a card does.
  • Teams that are about to receive a red card are already scoring 12–16 % less in the half hour before it, and their opponents more. Part of the measured effect is therefore the match situation that produces cards, not the card. A conservative causal reading is about −0.3 on the log scale rather than −0.45.

Why the effect is smaller than it looks

This is the part of the paper I find most useful. A naïve before/after comparison attributes everything to the card. The event study shows the pre-trend, which is the usual signature of endogeneity: cards happen to teams that are already being pushed back. The paper reports both the raw multipliers and the pre-trend-adjusted ones, and the value surface is recomputed under the conservative scenario.

What I did myself

The design (five-minute panel, nested model levels, held-out selection), the data checks, the backward recursion for the value function and the pricing in points, and the writing. The estimation code (an IRLS Poisson fit, the panel builder, the bootstrap) was built with AI tools. I trust it for three reasons: it recovers known parameters from 20,000 simulated matches to within two standard errors; the cluster-bootstrap standard errors agree with the analytic ones to within 5 %; and the headline multipliers match the one published benchmark I could find for this question to within sampling error.

Status and limits

Manuscript in preparation. Limits: the five-minute grid treats a goal and a card in the same interval as simultaneous; injury time is folded into the last interval; team quality is proxied by odds rather than modelled as a random effect; and the pre-trend adjustment is a bound, not an identification strategy.
